The Other Invasion

Something on this island was older than the war. And it had been waiting a long time for someone to arrive.

On the morning eleven thousand Marines came ashore, a woman on the high ground above the Lunga watched a basin of water move for the first time in a hundred and ten years. She was not the only one who noticed.

Three witnesses

One counts. One calculates. One watches.

A Marine sergeant, a Japanese fighter pilot and the last keeper of a family duty. Each of them sees something they cannot report. Pick one.

He counts

Sergeant Chris Calloway counts his twelve men at every halt. He has done it since Parris Island.

On the night of August 8, he stands on the beach at Lunga Point and watches the sea battle off Savo. He counts the ships. So do the men next to him.

Ensign Hiro Tetsuo Nakajima flies a Zero five hundred and sixty miles from Rabaul to Guadalcanal, and five hundred and sixty back.

She watches

Every morning of her life, Yael bat Miriam walks up to a stone on the high ground and looks at the water in it.

Five generations of her family have done the same at four places above the Lunga, and written down what they saw. Her mother told her the water would always be still, and that one day it would not.

Page after page after page of it, two and a half thousand entries, the most boring document in the Pacific.Chapter Three

From Chapter One

The camp was empty. The rice was still hot.

Calloway — Red Beach, Guadalcanal. August 7, 1942.

They found the camp at a little after two in the afternoon, on rising ground where a spur of the jungle broke and opened into a cleared patch maybe sixty yards across.

Ruiz was on point and put up a fist and everybody went flat, and Calloway came up beside him and looked through the ferns at a laborers' bivouac laid out as neat as a fairground.

Tents in two rows. Palm-thatch lean-tos with sleeping mats rolled and stacked. A cook fire in a stone ring at the centre, and a long plank table, and mosquito nets strung between poles. Someone had swept the ground. There were tracks in the swept dirt where the broom had gone, curved, overlapping, tidy.

They watched it for nine minutes. Calloway timed it on his wristwatch because there was nothing else to do with his hands. Nothing moved but the mosquito netting, which moved because there was a wind, and Calloway kept telling himself that: because there is a wind.

Then Hoyt sent two fire teams around the flanks and they went in, and it was empty, and it was worse than empty.

Rice in the pots over the fire ring. Calloway put the back of his fingers against the iron and pulled them away and shook his hand out and looked at Doc Merrigan and said, "It's hot."

"Fire's still got coals in it."

"It's hot, Doc."

There were bowls set out. Twenty, thirty bowls on the plank table, half of them with rice still in them, chopsticks laid across the rims or dropped where they fell. A tin cup on its side with tea still spreading out of it into the wood grain, still dark, still wet at the leading edge. Half a dozen bottles of Asahi beer standing in a row, three of them opened, one of them a quarter drunk and beaded with sweat. Cigarettes in a saucer, two of them burned down to the paper, one of them with an inch of ash holding its shape.

Dombrowski said it again from somewhere behind him. "Where are they?"

And the men started going through the tents, because that's what men do, and the tents were full of everything a man owns. Boots, paired and set out. Shaving kits. A shirt half-mended with the needle still stuck through it, threaded. Photographs — a woman in a garden, a boy in a school uniform, an old couple sitting very straight in a studio somewhere with a painted backdrop of a mountain. Letters in a language none of them could read, folded and stacked and tied with string. A calendar with dates crossed off in red pencil up to and including today.

Two-Bit Harris found a phonograph and got as far as putting his hand on the crank before Calloway said his name once and he took his hand off it.

"Sarge, they ran," Bickel said. He was holding somebody's toothbrush. He seemed unaware he was holding it. "The Navy shelled the hell out of them and they ran and left their chow, that's all this is."

"Sure."

"That's all it is."

Calloway walked the perimeter of the clearing instead of answering, and that was when he found the thing that he did not tell anyone about, not that day and not for a long time after.

At the western edge of the camp the swept ground stopped in a clean line where the jungle started, and the tracks went out of the camp into the trees — the pressed-down grass, the churned mud at the lip of the treeline, the mess that two or three hundred men make when they leave a place in a hurry. That was all correct. That was what running looked like.

But the mud at the treeline had been walked back over.

Not many. Not a crowd. One set, maybe two, coming out of the jungle toward the camp, laid down over the top of the outbound mess, unhurried — the prints even and square and evenly spaced, the way a man walks when he has all the time in the world. They came out of the trees, crossed the churned ground, and stopped eight feet in, on the swept dirt, where the broom marks were.

And then there was nothing. No prints going on. No prints going back. The swept dirt was smooth for the next fifty yards to the fire ring, curved and overlapping and tidy, and no one had walked across it at all.

Calloway stood there in the heat with the flies starting to find the rice, and looked at the last two prints in the mud, and did the only useful thing he could think of.

He turned around and counted his squad.

Twelve.

He counted them again anyway.
